01 / Brief

The business question was conversion. Our job was to investigate experience friction.

The client engagement began around concerns about traffic and conversion. We did not have evidence that any single UX issue caused those business outcomes, so the audit focused on identifying plausible usability and decision-making friction across the homepage and checkout journey rather than claiming causation.

This was an audit, not a redesign. The recommendations shown in the original deliverable were proposed directions and were not subsequently validated through a shipped Pepperfry experience.
02 / Approach

Multiple lenses, one shopping journey.

The collaborative team combined expert evaluation with lightweight qualitative observation. My individual contribution covered the homepage audit, competitor analysis, participation in the walkthrough sessions, synthesis and presentation.

Three informal participants reinforced the same broad concern.

During walkthroughs, participants described the direction of the experience as confusing, found the amount of competing information distracting, and raised confidence/credibility concerns while trying to make decisions. With only three informal participants, these observations are directional evidence—not a representative usability study.

03 / Synthesis

The problem wasn't simply “too much UI.” It was too much to process, with too little confidence at decision points.

Across the audit, individual issues began to cluster into three larger experience problems. That synthesis made the recommendations easier to prioritize than treating every heuristic observation as equally important.

Reduce cognitive load

Consolidate header/navigation complexity, reduce competing calls to action and make category structure easier to scan.

Increase decision confidence

Use stronger ratings, review context and authentic social proof around products and testimonials.

Shorten the path

Reduce unnecessary scrolling and repeated information, and make progress through checkout more obvious.

04 / Homepage findings

Discovery competed with navigation, promotion and information density.

Three headers became one hierarchy problem.

The audit identified multiple headers as a source of clutter and recommended consolidating essential actions—brand, search, profile and cart—while moving secondary utility links away from the primary shopping path.

Categories mixed different ways of thinking.

Rooms, materials and price tiers appeared together in navigation. We recommended separating primary room categories from secondary style/material filters to create a more predictable hierarchy.

Promotions competed with products.

Multiple calls to action, auto-rotating hero content and dense informational sections could dilute purchase intent. Recommendations prioritized product visibility, clearer promotional hierarchy and more meaningful browsing filters.

05 / Confidence

Furniture is a considered purchase. The experience needed stronger reasons to trust the decision.

The audit repeatedly returned to credibility: static testimonials and limited review context did not provide enough evidence for a high-consideration purchase. Recommendations included clearer rating breakdowns, review counts, verified product ratings and richer real-world product storytelling.

Signal

Ratings

Expose useful rating and review counts where users compare products rather than making social proof peripheral.

Signal

Testimonials

Move beyond isolated text quotes toward evidence that feels attributable and connected to actual products.

Signal

Context

Show furniture in meaningful real-life settings so users can better understand fit, use and quality before purchase.

06 / Checkout

As purchase intent increased, the journey still demanded too much navigation.

The checkout audit identified long scrolling, oversized cards, repeated information, difficult item selection, multiple sign-up steps and weak previous/next orientation. The recommendation direction was consolidation: keep essential checkout information together, compact secondary detail and make progression explicit.

01

Compress detail

Use more compact cards and expandable sections so key product and purchase information appears earlier.

02

Clarify progression

Use obvious previous/back and next/continue actions so users know where they are and what happens next.

03

Consolidate checkout

Reduce repeated information and unnecessary transitions, keeping the purchase task as continuous as possible.

07 / Competitive reference

Competitors were references, not templates.

We reviewed patterns from Myntra, Flipkart, Westside, Amazon and IKEA to compare how established commerce experiences handled navigation, editorial merchandising, filters, reviews and checkout continuity. Those patterns informed recommendations, but the goal was to diagnose Pepperfry's specific friction rather than copy another retailer's interface.

Outcome & limits

The deliverable was a diagnosis, not proof of conversion impact.

The audit was presented as recommendations to the client. We did not receive follow-up feedback after delivery, did not redesign or ship the experience, and did not measure conversion after the recommendations. The value of the project is therefore in the evaluation process, prioritization and reasoning—not an unverified business outcome.

What I carried forward: a useful audit should do more than list violations. It should connect individual observations into a smaller set of experience problems that a product team can actually prioritize.
